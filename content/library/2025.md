In 2025 I read 72 books. The year had a clear centre of gravity: **AI and economic statecraft** — how intelligence gets built and commercialised, and how money and technology become instruments of power — anchored by a return to **Brazilian literature** and a couple of books that read like literature about science. These ten left the strongest impression.

## Best books I've read in 2025

### *Como os Países Quebram* (How Countries Go Broke) by Ray Dalio

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122596213/COMO_OS_PASES_QUEBRAM_1755520196122596213SK-V11755520196B.jpg)

Dalio applies his "big debt cycle" lens to sovereigns: how great powers accumulate unsustainable debt, what the endgame mechanics look like, and the warning signs along the way. Read in Portuguese.

A clear, almost clinical walk through deficits, reserve-currency erosion and the long arc of fiscal decline. The natural companion to his *Changing World Order* — read them together.

### *Underground Empire* by Henry Farrell & Abraham Newman

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122541807/UNDERGROUND_EMPIRE_1737731543122541807SK-V11737731543B.jpg)

How the United States quietly turned the plumbing of the global economy — dollar clearing, SWIFT, semiconductors, undersea cables — into instruments of coercion.

The best single book I read on **economic statecraft**: sanctions, export controls and chokepoints explained as infrastructure. Essential for understanding the news.

### *Coisa de Rico* by Michel Alcoforado

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122588300/COISA_DE_RICO_1752528525122588300SK-V11752528526B.jpg)

An anthropologist's ethnography of the Brazilian ultra-rich — how money, taste and distinction actually work among the *endinheirados*.

Rigorous social science that reads like sharp reportage. Funny, uncomfortable, and revealing about a world rarely studied from the inside.

### *Dom Casmurro* by Machado de Assis

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/180/DOM_CASMURRO_1605571368180SK1605571392B.jpg)

The Brazilian canonical novel: Bentinho, Capitu, jealousy, and the most famous unreliable narrator in the language. A reread that rewards every return.

Did Capitu betray him, or is the whole book the self-justification of a jealous man? Machado never tells you — and that's the genius.

### *Supremacy: AI, ChatGPT, and the Race That Will Change the World* by Parmy Olson

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122537050/SUPREMACY_AIN_CHATGPT_AND_THE_1736197628122537050SK-V11736197628B.jpg)

The story of the modern AI race told through Sam Altman (OpenAI) and Demis Hassabis (DeepMind) — two opposed temperaments and the commercial pressures that reshaped the field.

The clearest narrative I've read of *how we got here*. For an engineer working on AI, useful context on the incentives behind the tools.

### *Main Street Millionaire* by Codie Sanchez

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122468398/MAIN_STREET_MILLIONAIRE_1719221433122468398SK-V11719221433B.jpg)

A contrarian wealth playbook: buy "boring," cash-flowing small businesses instead of chasing startups.

Refreshingly concrete after a decade of tech-glamour narratives. Acquisition entrepreneurship as a path most people never consider.

### *A República Tecnológica* (The Technological Republic) by Alexander C. Karp & Nicholas Zamiska

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122554998/A_REPUBLICA_TECNOLOGICA_1741611274122554998SK-V11741611274B.jpg)

The Palantir CEO's argument that Silicon Valley abandoned hard, national-purpose problems for consumer trifles, and should re-ally itself with the West and the state.

I rated it 3★ and I **strongly disagree** with its worldview — but it earns a place here precisely because it lays out, in their own words, the ideology of a powerful group that is now deeply influential in the U.S. government. Read it to understand what they believe and where it leads, not as a program to endorse.

### *The Magic of Code* by Samuel Arbesman

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122596518/THE_MAGIC_OF_CODE_1755617197122596518SK-V11755617197B.jpg)

Code as a new kind of language — a humanistic, wonder-driven tour of computation across science, art and the humanities.

A reminder of *why* this craft is fascinating, beyond the day job. The rare tech book that's about awe rather than productivity.

### *When We Cease to Understand the World* by Benjamín Labatut

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/11815393/WHEN_WE_CEASE_TO_UNDERSTAND_TH_161944611011815393SK-V11619446111B.jpg)

A hypnotic blend of fact and fiction about the vertiginous edge of 20th-century science — Schwarzschild, Grothendieck, Heisenberg — where genius shades into madness.

Literature about science that left me unsettled for days. Beautiful and strange. My favourite *reading experience* of the year.

### *One Day, Everyone Will Have Always Been Against This* by Omar El Akkad

![](https://skoob.s3.amazonaws.com/livros/122493519/ONE_DAYN_EVERYONE_WILL_HAVE_AL_1724759018122493519SK-V11724759019B.jpg)

A searing essay on moral complicity, witness, and the contradictions of Western liberalism in the face of atrocity — told from the side of the oppressed.

This is the most heartbreaking book I read all year. I finished it with a deep, lingering sadness that stayed with me for days. It's also one of the most important: it forces you to see current world events through the eyes of those who suffer them, not those who narrate them from a safe distance. Hard to read, and necessary.
